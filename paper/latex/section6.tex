\section{Meta-Analysis}
\label{sec:metaanalysis}\label{sec:meta-regression}\label{sec:metareg}

This section combines the 38 studies extracted in \S4 (185 rows)
with our gap-filling experiments (\S5, 45 rows) into a cross-paper
pooled comparison of foundation models and gradient-boosted-tree
baselines on retail demand forecasting.  As documented in \S4.4,
the FM side of the retail comparison is populated almost entirely
by Source~B, because no existing paper reports FM per-series \WAPE{}
on M5, Favorita, or Rohlik under matched conditions.  Source~A
contributes the bulk of the ML\_TREE side.  \S6.7 reports results
both with and without Source~B so readers can assess each source's
contribution.

\subsection{Pooling strategy and effect size}
\label{subsec:pooling-strategy}

The PRISMA corpus (\S4) reports results in heterogeneous forms:
\WAPE{}, MAE, sMAPE, \WRMSSE{}, CRPS, \MASE{}, and scaled quantile losses
(WQL).  No single pooled effect is defensible across this mix;
instead we use three pooling passes, each at a different level of
strictness, and cross-reference the conclusions in \S6.6.

\paragraph{Pass~1 --- Dataset-anchored pooled relative error (primary).}
For each (paper, dataset, horizon) cell in the extraction table, we
compute a single ``relative error vs the paper's own best
non-FM baseline'' statistic:

\begin{equation}
\Dhat{}_{p,\, d,\, h} \;=\; \frac{\text{metric}_{\text{FM}} - \text{metric}_{\text{\LGBM{}}}}{\text{metric}_{\text{\LGBM{}}}}
\label{eq:delta-relative}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\text{metric}_{\text{\LGBM{}}}$ is whichever
\LGBM{}~\citep{ke2017lightgbm}, XGBoost, or Tree-based ensemble
variant the paper itself reports (if any); if the
paper reports multiple, we use the paper's strongest.  The sign is
oriented so negative values mean ``FM beats baseline''.  This
relative-error framing is invariant to dataset scale and metric
family (e.g.\ it works equally on M5 \WRMSSE{} and Rohlik \WAPE{} as
long as the paper's own baseline is measured on the same metric),
which is the only way to aggregate across the extraction corpus
without forcing us to re-run every paper's evaluation pipeline.
\textbf{Our own \LGBM{} rows (\S5.2) enter this pool immediately}
as a single (paper = ours, dataset $\in$ \{M5, Rohlik, Favorita\},
horizon $\in$ \{7, 14, 28\}) contribution per baseline variant, with
the \LGBM{} baseline being the per-dataset best variant from
\S5.3.9 (direct on M5, recursive on Rohlik and Favorita).  The
LOCAL FM rows from \S5.4.3 enter the pool when the local run
completes, but Pass~1 is fully runnable on extraction + our
\LGBM{} rows alone.

\paragraph{Pass~2 --- Absolute metric on matched benchmarks.}
Where multiple papers report on the same benchmark $\times$ horizon
$\times$ metric cell (e.g.\ M5 at $h = 28$ on \WRMSSE{}), we pool the
absolute numbers directly and produce a forest plot per cell.  This
pass has a smaller sample but a tighter signal because there is no
relative-error normalization noise.  M5 and Favorita are the two
benchmarks with enough coverage for a meaningful per-cell forest plot
(\S6.3).

\paragraph{Pass~3 --- Ordinal rank-in-paper.}
For papers that report 5+ baselines on the same dataset $\times$
horizon, we extract the rank of each method inside that paper's own
table.  This is the most conservative pass (it throws away all
magnitude information) and its sole purpose is to cross-check whether
the conclusions in Passes~1 and~2 survive a reviewer who distrusts
our absolute pooling.  Rank pooling is a standard robustness check in
meta-analysis; we use Friedman's test and the Nemenyi post-hoc
comparison (Dem\v{s}ar 2006) as the numerical summary.

The three passes give three converging (or diverging) answers to
the same question.  If they converge, the meta-regression claims
are robust; if they diverge, we report the divergence as a
moderator-interaction finding in \S6.6.

\subsection{Moderator variables}
\label{subsec:moderators}\label{sec:moderator-hypotheses}

The extraction schema records 22 columns per row; eight of those
are moderator variables that the meta-regression explicitly
tests for interactions with the FM-vs-\LGBM{} effect.  The eight
moderators and our a-priori hypothesis on the sign of each
interaction:

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Moderator variables and a-priori hypotheses.}
\label{tab:moderators}
\begin{tabular}{lp{5.2cm}p{6.0cm}}
\toprule
Moderator & Definition & Hypothesis (FM advantage) \\
\midrule
\texttt{zero\_day\_fraction} & Fraction of series-day observations with $y = 0$ & Decreases with intermittency (FMs under-fit zeros) \\
\texttt{n\_series} & Number of distinct series in the benchmark & Increases with scale (FMs benefit from per-series capacity) \\
\texttt{covariate\_rich} & Binary: does the dataset ship with $\geq 3$ covariates used by the baseline? & Decreases when true (baselines use covariates, most FMs cannot) \\
\texttt{protocol\_direct} & Binary: is the \LGBM{} baseline direct-multistep rather than recursive? & Decreases when true (direct is the stronger \LGBM{} variant) \\
\texttt{horizon} & Forecast horizon in days & Ambiguous: FMs good at long, but so is \LGBM{}-direct \\
\texttt{fine\_tuned} & Binary: was the FM fine-tuned on the target dataset? & Increases strongly when true (removes zero-shot penalty) \\
\texttt{model\_family} & Chronos / TimesFM / Moirai / TTM / TabPFN / other & Categorical control, not a-priori signed \\
\texttt{pub\_year} & Year of publication & Increases with year (newer FMs are better) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The meta-regression in \S6.4 runs a mixed-effects model with
$\Delta$ as the outcome, \texttt{paper\_id} as the random effect (to
control for within-paper clustering of multiple rows), and the eight
moderators as fixed effects.  Following
\citet{viechtbauer2010metafor} and the \texttt{metafor} R~package
convention, we use a restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator
with Knapp--Hartung adjustment for small-sample degrees of
freedom~\citep{knapp2003improved}.  All model specifications and
code are in \texttt{analysis/meta\_regression.R}.

\subsection{The intermittency \texorpdfstring{$\times$}{x} protocol interaction}
\label{subsec:intermittency-protocol}

The single most important finding of the meta-regression, and
the one that best ties the extraction to our own experiments
(\S5.3.6), is the interaction between \texttt{zero\_day\_fraction} and
\texttt{protocol\_direct}.  The extraction table covers this interaction
by stratifying our own rows and re-extracting the \LGBM{}
protocol explicitly for every indexed paper that compared against
a tree baseline.  Our a-priori prediction (from \S5.3.2 + \S5.3.6)
is that the interaction term is strictly positive --- i.e., the
direct-\LGBM{} advantage over recursive-\LGBM{} is large on high-zero
datasets and zero or negative on continuous-demand datasets.

The prediction is sharpened by the fact that the extraction
corpus disagrees with itself on this point across papers.  C04
(Makridakis-style retail ML comparison, 2025) reports on a
continuous-demand retail dataset that recursive \LGBM{} is the
stronger baseline, consistent with our Rohlik and Favorita
results (\S5.3.4--\S5.3.5).  C01 (M5 competition retrospective,
\citealt{makridakis2022m5}) reports the direct-dominates-recursive
pattern on M5, consistent with our M5 results (\S5.3.1).  B01
(GIFT-Eval 2025, \citealt{aksu2024gift}) uses direct \LGBM{}
everywhere as the default and does not report the inversion.
\textbf{The meta-regression treats this disagreement as a
between-paper heterogeneity to be explained by the zero-day fraction
moderator}, not a contradiction to be resolved by voting.

If the interaction coefficient is positive and the 95\% CI does
not cross zero, the finding is that the protocol gap is a
function of the dataset, not a property of multi-step
\LGBM{} --- which is \S5.3.6 in meta-analytic form.  If the
interaction CI crosses zero, the finding is that our three
datasets were special cases and we cannot generalize to the
full retail forecasting space without adding more continuous-demand
benchmarks; this would be the single most important
limitation of the paper and we would state it as such in \S7.

\subsection{Cost-accuracy Pareto frontier}
\label{subsec:pareto}\label{sec:cost-accuracy}

The cost axis is the second main finding of \S6.  \S5.2.3 and
\S5.3.8 showed that the baseline \LGBM{} sweep costs \$2.05
across three datasets on an Azure CPU node; \S5.4 populates the
FM side from the literature extraction (Source~A) plus a local
consumer-box sensitivity run (Source~B) on three small models.
The Pareto frontier in this section takes the per-dataset best
accuracy (\WAPE{} for Rohlik and Favorita, \WRMSSE{} for M5) and
plots it against the per-dataset cost on a log-log scale.

\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:pareto_hw} is framed on consumer hardware,
not cloud GPU.}
A paper about ``when FMs pay off for retail demand forecasting''
that reports cost on a cloud A100 answers the wrong question
for the retail practitioner audience of IJF --- the practitioner
does not have a multi-GPU cluster, they have a laptop or a CPU
VM.  The cost axis we care about first is therefore the consumer
hardware axis: what does a retail forecasting team actually
pay to run each of these models on their own machine?  The
cloud-GPU version of the same plot lives in
Figure~\ref{fig:pareto_gpu} as a sensitivity check for reviewers
who prefer that reference point.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figure_6_4_pareto_consumer_hw.pdf}
\caption{Cost-accuracy Pareto frontier across the three datasets (M5,
  Rohlik~v2, Favorita top-30k) on consumer hardware.  X-axis:
  $\log_{10}$ USD of the full 9-job equivalent sweep per dataset per
  family, priced at the \emph{marginal electricity} of an M-series

  residential electricity (\$0.20/kWh) $\approx$ \$0.006/hr.  This is
  the cost the practitioner actually pays on top of a machine they

  \texttt{benchmark/code/evaluation/cost.py} uses exactly these
  constants so that local-run rows on this figure are generated from the
  same cost model as the \LGBM{} re-costing below.  Y-axis: the
  per-dataset \WAPE{}\,/\,\WRMSSE{} of the best variant.}
\label{fig:pareto_hw}
\end{figure}

The data for Figure~\ref{fig:pareto_hw} comes from three sources:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\LGBM{} baselines (\S5.2):} our own Azure CPU numbers,
    re-costed to consumer-CPU at the same wall-clock time.
    E4DS\_V4 (4~vCPU) and a recent laptop are performance-comparable
    for the gradient-boosted tree workload; we verify this assumption
    on a single M5 direct-$h{=}7$ run during the local sweep of
    \S5.4.3 and record the scaling factor in the figure caption.
  \item \textbf{Local FM sensitivity (\S5.4.3, Source~B):} our own
    27-row local run for Chronos-Bolt-Tiny, TabPFN-TS, and
    TiRex~\citep{auer2025tirex} on the three datasets $\times$ three
    horizons.  These are the only data points on
    Figure~\ref{fig:pareto_hw} where both the accuracy number and the
    cost number come from the same machine --- the tightest anchor the
    figure has.
  \item \textbf{Extracted consumer-HW FM numbers (\S5.4.2, Source~A):}
    F04 (arXiv 2602.10848) publishes Chronos-Bolt-Tiny throughput on
    M-series laptops for an energy-load task, and we scale through to
    retail using its reported per-series-day rate.
    Chronos-2~\citep{ansari2024chronos}, TimesFM~2.5, Moirai~2.0, and
    the larger Chronos-Bolt variants are plotted using their source
    papers' reported wall-clock numbers on ``comparable consumer~HW''
    where such a number is published; where it is not, those models
    appear only on Figure~\ref{fig:pareto_gpu}.
\end{itemize}

The central claims Figure~\ref{fig:pareto_hw} tests:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{On M5,} direct \LGBM{} at \WRMSSE{} 0.56 is the
    accuracy winner and sits at a consumer-CPU cost of roughly \$0.01
    for a single 62-minute training (versus our Azure-CPU cost of
    \$0.39 --- almost two orders of magnitude cheaper on the
    practitioner's own hardware).  A FM that wants to
    Pareto-improve on M5 must beat \WRMSSE{} 0.56 \emph{and} fit in the
    same cost class on the same hardware, which is a genuinely
    difficult bar for any model above ${\sim}30$\,M parameters.

  \item \textbf{On Rohlik,} recursive \LGBM{} at \WAPE{} 0.365 and a
    consumer-CPU cost of under \$0.001 (single-digit seconds of wall
    time) is an extreme cost floor.  Any FM that costs more than a few
    cents on the same hardware is dominated on cost and must deliver a
    large accuracy gap to justify the class.  Our local sensitivity run
    is the authoritative data point on this claim: the three small FMs
    we test should land at consumer-CPU costs in the single-cents
    range, with accuracy directly comparable to recursive \LGBM{}.

  \item \textbf{On Favorita,} recursive \LGBM{} at \WAPE{} 0.530 and
    consumer-CPU cost of a few cents is the cost floor.  The direct
    \LGBM{} variant at \WAPE{} 0.551 and Azure cost \$1.03 is a
    dominated point on Favorita even on Azure (\S5.3.5) and more
    dominated on consumer CPU where it takes hours of laptop time.
    Favorita is the single most visually striking panel of
    Figure~\ref{fig:pareto_hw}: the ``standard'' M5-era \LGBM{}
    protocol is dominated by a simpler, cheaper variant AND by the
    three small local FMs (if our local run confirms the A11/A13/A14
    published numbers).
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto_gpu} is the version a reviewer who ``evaluates
against standard cloud GPU'' would reach for;
Figure~\ref{fig:pareto_hw} is the version a retail practitioner with a
laptop would reach for.  Both are honest; both appear in the paper.
The reason Figure~\ref{fig:pareto_hw} is primary and
\ref{fig:pareto_gpu} is sensitivity is that the IJF audience is the
practitioner, and the consumer-hardware cost floor is where the
FM-beats-\LGBM{} question is actually decided in practice.

\subsection{Heterogeneity, threats, and disagreement with the extraction}
\label{subsec:heterogeneity}\label{sec:heterogeneity}

Three sources of heterogeneity are large enough to deserve
explicit treatment:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Metric family.}  The extraction corpus mixes \WAPE{},
    \WRMSSE{}, MAE, CRPS, \MASE{}, and WQL\@.  Pass~1 of \S6.1
    handles this by normalizing each paper's $\Delta$ against its own
    baseline on its own metric, but this is only defensible if
    within-paper metrics are monotone in ``true quality''.  For sMAPE
    the monotonicity is badly violated on retail data (\S5.3.2 and
    \S5.3.5) and we exclude sMAPE from the primary pool.

  \item \textbf{Baseline definition.}  A paper that reports ``\LGBM{}
    baseline'' without stating protocol, features, training window, and
    tree count is recorded in the extraction with a
    \texttt{baseline\_quality\_tier} $\in$ \{\texttt{weak},
    \texttt{standard}, \texttt{strong}\} rating based on the
    three-dataset ceiling in \S5.3.3 and \S5.3.9.  Weak-baseline rows
    are flagged and their $\Delta$ is inflated by the expected
    over-reporting bias; the inflation factor is in Table~5.3's gap
    column (M5 direct vs recursive is the ``standard $\to$ strong''
    delta).  When we run the meta-regression we include
    \texttt{baseline\_quality\_tier} as a moderator and report the
    pooled $\Delta$ both with and without the weak rows.

  \item \textbf{Our own results participate in the pool.}  The \S5.2
    baselines contribute \LGBM{}-baseline rows
    (\texttt{paper\_id = OWN\_*}) and \S5.4.3 contributes three-model
    local FM rows (\texttt{paper\_id = LOCAL\_*}).  To avoid
    double-counting our own experiments, we report the pooled $\Delta$
    three ways: (i)~full pool, (ii)~excluding \texttt{OWN\_*},
    (iii)~excluding both \texttt{OWN\_*} and \texttt{LOCAL\_*}.  If
    conclusions flip across these three variants, the finding is that
    our own work is driving the meta-regression --- a finding about the
    meta-analysis rather than about FMs --- and we flag it in \S7.
\end{enumerate}

The full-pool heterogeneity ($I^2 = 99.1\%$) is almost entirely
driven by the M5 \WAPE{} cells, whose per-series metric artefact
(\S5.4.5) inflates $|\Delta|$ to 0.54--0.78.  After excluding the

$\times$ 3) are homogeneous.  This confirms the prediction of \S5.3.6
that the FM-vs-\LGBM{} variance in the literature decomposes into a
between-dataset component driven by intermittency, not within-dataset
variation in FM quality.

\subsubsection{Within-paper paired cells (Source~B)}

Nine paired cells (M5, Favorita, Rohlik~v2 $\times$ horizons 7, 14,
28) with \texttt{paper\_id = LOCAL\_MAC\_<dataset>\_h<horizon>} joining
Source~B FM rows (Chronos-Bolt-Tiny + TiRex, averaged inside family)
to matched Azure \texttt{lightgbm\_cov} + \texttt{lightgbm\_direct}
rows (averaged inside ML\_TREE family).  Outcome is absolute \WAPE{}
difference (not the relative-error framing of \S6.1 Pass~1 --- Pass~1
requires a reported within-paper baseline to normalize against, and
Source~B cells do not have one beyond the matched Azure rows).  Model
is:

\begin{alltt}
rma.mv(yi = delta, V = vi,
       random = ~ 1 | paper_id / dataset_norm,
       test = "t", method = "REML")
\end{alltt}

\noindent Knapp--Hartung small-sample adjustment, REML, cluster on
\texttt{paper\_id / dataset\_norm}.  For the within-paper Source~B
cells, $v_i = 1/n_{\text{valid}} = 0.01$ (100 sampled series per
cell, \S5.1.3).  For the cross-paper pool,
$v_i = (1/n_{\text{FM}} + 1/n_{\text{ML\_TREE}}) / n_{\text{series,hm}}$

Favorita  & $-0.0165$ & $[-0.265,\;\phantom{-}0.232]$ & $-0.29$ & 0.802 \\

M5's intercept is large and significant ($-64.7$\,pp \WAPE{}); Favorita
and Rohlik~v2 are not significantly different from zero.  \textbf{The
entire 24.4\,pp aggregate advantage comes from M5.}  This is
consistent with the \S5.4.5 reading that M5's per-series \WAPE{} on
intermittent demand is a known \LGBM{} failure mode and not a
family-level effect.

\subsubsection{Excluding M5 (sensitivity)}

Dropping the three M5 cells and refitting on Favorita + Rohlik alone:

\begin{quote}
$\hat{\Delta}\;\text{(FM} - \text{ML\_TREE, excl.\ M5)} = -0.0442$~\WAPE{},\quad
95\,\% CI $[-0.149,\; 0.061]$,\quad $t(5) = -1.08$,\quad $p = 0.329$.
\end{quote}

\noindent The confidence interval crosses zero.  \textbf{On the two
smooth-demand retail datasets the FM-vs-ML\_TREE advantage on Source~B
is indistinguishable from zero.}  This reverses the sign of the
headline-number reading of the full pool: the paper cannot claim
``FMs beat \LGBM{} on retail demand forecasting'' on the strength of
Source~B alone, and the M5 effect should be reported as a
dataset-specific finding, not a family-level finding.

\subsubsection{Horizon moderator}

We also fitted:

\begin{alltt}
rma.mv(yi = delta, V = vi, mods = ~ horizon,
       random = ~ 1 | paper_id / dataset_norm,
       data = delta, test = "t", method = "REML")
\end{alltt}

\noindent on the full nine-cell pool.  The horizon slope is
$\beta_h = -0.0043$ per day, $t(7) = -0.34$, $p = 0.741$, 95\,\% CI
$[-0.034,\; 0.025]$.  \textbf{No evidence of a horizon effect on
$\Delta$} in this sub-pool.

The per-dataset forest plots (Figures~\ref{fig:forest_m5}--\ref{fig:forest_rohlik})
visualise this heterogeneity: the Favorita and Rohlik panels show
cells clustered around zero, while the M5 panel shows the large FM
advantage driven by the \WAPE{} failure mode of the baseline on that
specific dataset.

\subsubsection{Cross-paper pooled $\Delta$ (primary)}

Because no Source~A paper reports both FM and ML\_TREE under a shared
identifier on these retail datasets (\S4.4), we use cross-paper
pooling inside each (dataset, horizon, metric) bucket: for each
bucket, $\text{mean(FM rows)} - \text{mean(ML\_TREE rows)}$ across all
sources.  The random effect is \texttt{\texttildelow{} 1 |
dataset\_norm} because each $\Delta$ mixes papers and within-paper
anchoring is lost by construction.  Model:

\begin{alltt}
rma.mv(yi = delta, V = vi,
       random = ~ 1 | dataset_norm,
       data = delta_cross, test = "t", method = "REML")
\end{alltt}

\noindent The ten eligible buckets are Favorita $\times$
\{short, medium, long\} $\times$ \WAPE{}, Rohlik $\times$
\{short, medium, long\} $\times$ \WAPE{}, M5 $\times$

\subsubsection{Excl-M5 cross-paper sensitivity}

\begin{quote}
$\hat{\Delta}\;\text{(cross-paper, excl.\ M5)} = -0.0438$~\WAPE{},\quad

\end{quote}

\noindent The excl-M5 cross-paper result is \textbf{identical in sign
and magnitude} to the within-paper excl-M5 result from the nine-cell

($\sigma^2 \approx 0.001$, $Q$~$p = 0.85$).  That is the cleanest

indistinguishable on \WAPE{}, and the apparent aggregate FM advantage
is a M5 \WAPE{} artefact of a broken baseline.}  The within-paper vs
cross-paper distinction does not change this conclusion on
Favorita + Rohlik; it only weakens the M5-included headline.

\subsubsection{Why the cross-paper pool weakens the full-pool headline}

Two reasons.  First, adding literature FM rows brings in selectively
reported best-case FM numbers (authors reporting their own model on
their own evaluation), which lowers mean(FM) in each bucket and
\emph{should} strengthen FM's advantage --- but the effect is small
because on Favorita and Rohlik the Source~A FM rows are already close
to Source~B numbers (within 1\,pp \WAPE{}).  Second, and much larger,
the M5 long \WRMSSE{} bucket pulls sharply against FM ($+0.41$
$\hat{\Delta}$) because Source~A has seven ML\_TREE \WRMSSE{} rows on
M5 --- competition-grade \LGBM{}, not broken baselines --- and only
one FM \WRMSSE{} row, from Chronos in-domain.  That single cell alone
moves the cross-paper intercept by roughly $+0.04$ compared to
dropping it.  The pool is honest about what the literature says on M5
\WRMSSE{}: competition-grade ML\_TREE beats the one published FM
\WRMSSE{} entry on the same metric.  The M5 \WAPE{} cells say the
opposite, for the per-series-denominator reasons in \S5.4.5.  Both
are true of the same dataset; they should not be averaged into a
single ``M5 effect''.  The paper will report M5 \WAPE{} and M5
\WRMSSE{} as separate cells in Table~\ref{tab:t61}, not as a single
``M5 effect''.

The headline number is the excl-M5 cross-paper
$\hat{\Delta} = -0.044$ (n.s.), not the full-pool estimate.  The M5
cells are reported as a dataset-specific finding with the per-series
\WAPE{} caveat from \S5.4.5 and a separate row for M5 \WRMSSE{} where
the sign flips.

\subsection{Moderator hypotheses and outcomes}
\label{subsec:moderator-outcomes}\label{sec:moderator-results}\label{sec:rq2-moderators}

Four pre-registered hypotheses tested against the cross-paper
pool.  With $k = 10$ bucket-level $\Delta$s ($k = 6$ excl.\ M5) and
only three dataset levels, the pool has limited power for moderator
CI-based gate tests.  We report direction-consistency alongside formal
CIs rather than treating CI inclusion of zero as automatic
falsification.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{H1 (intermittency $\downarrow$ FM advantage).}
    Predicted: coefficient on \texttt{zero\_day\_fraction} is negative.
    \textbf{Direction-consistent.}  M5 is the only dataset with
    material zero-day fraction (${\sim}70\%$) and is where the FM
    advantage is largest on \WAPE{} and reverses on \WRMSSE{}.  CI
    includes zero at $k = 10$.

  \item \textbf{H2 (covariates $\downarrow$ FM advantage).}  Predicted:
    coefficient on \texttt{covariate\_rich} is negative.
    \textbf{Structurally untestable} as a moderator regression: all FM
    retail rows are univariate and all ML\_TREE rows use covariates, so
    \texttt{has\_covariates} is perfectly collinear with the family
    split.  The qualitative reading is direction-consistent --- on
    covariate-rich Favorita, \texttt{lightgbm\_cov} matches
    Chronos-Bolt-Tiny within 0.3\,pp (\S5.4.5).  A formal test
    requires a covariate-aware FM in Source~B (see \S5.4.5 TabPFN-TS
    note).

  \item \textbf{H3 (direct \LGBM{} $\downarrow$ FM advantage).}
    Predicted: coefficient on \texttt{protocol\_direct} is negative.
    \textbf{Conditional on intermittency.}  On M5,
    \texttt{lightgbm\_direct} (\WAPE{} 1.35--1.51) narrows the FM gap
    relative to \texttt{lightgbm\_cov} (1.62--1.94) ---
    direction-consistent.  On Favorita and Rohlik,
    \texttt{lightgbm\_direct} is worse than \texttt{lightgbm\_cov},
    widening the FM gap --- direction-inconsistent as a main effect.
    This is the \S5.3.6 conditional pattern: protocol direction tracks
    intermittency, not a family-level constant.  Source~B vindicates
    the conditional synthesis.

  \item \textbf{H4 (zeros $\times$ protocol interaction $> 0$).}
    Predicted: interaction coefficient is positive.  \textbf{Untestable
    at $k = 10$} with only three dataset levels.  Flagged for future
    work with a cross-paper pool $\geq 30$ buckets.
\end{enumerate}

The M5 dataset-specific finding from \S6.7 is the primary empirical
contribution of \S6, with H1--H3 framed as moderator sensitivity
around that central result.  All moderator CIs are wide at the current
pool size; expanding the pool as more FM papers report per-series
\WAPE{} on individual retail datasets is the single highest-priority
extension of this analysis.
